      Side A: one session

      Notes slide toward a line on two lanes. When a Scratch note reaches it, flick the record up and down; for a Drop note, turn the record counterclockwise.

      Every hit is judged on timing (Perfect, Ok, Early or Miss) and builds score and combo. A session runs from two training rounds into a performance, then a freestyle.

      Side B: making it

      1. B1

        The idea

        I first wanted a 2D rhythm-action game in the spirit of Hi-Fi RUSH, but that genre leans on top-tier art and music. Looking at similar games, I landed on DJ scratching instead: fresh as a mechanic, and cheap to build. I started with a PC prototype using mouse-controlled scratching, then grew it into a small studio with a turntable and a mixing console. The goal was for players to feel they’re shaping the music, with every movement getting an immediate, satisfying sound.

      2. B2

        Building it

        After studying DJ scratching tutorials, I built a turntable model and made the record draggable with the mouse. That became the foundation. I recorded my own voice in FL Studio for the scratch sample, picked a free-to-use backing track, then designed the rhythm judgment track and the UI.

      3. B3

        Playtest and iterate

        I went through analyze, design, implement and playtest twice, at the Gold Spike and Gold milestones. In the first playtest, players didn’t know what to do. Clearer guidance and the rhythm judgment track made the game far easier to read, and more fun. My takeaway: get something playable and easy to understand in front of people before polishing difficult features.

      4. B4

        Under the hood

        Scratch recognition and scoring live in reusable classes. Lesson cues and thresholds are stored in a ScratchLesson asset, so lessons can be tuned without touching gameplay code, and automated checks run the same logic the game does. Every mixer knob and slider shares one control component instead of each needing its own script.
